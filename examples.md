# Examples

One worked illustration of the refusal gate that protects this skill's most distinctive, most skippable discipline: debunks count as findings. This example runs a single popular claim through the full tier taxonomy and ends in a debunk — not a confirmation — because that's the case most likely to get quietly dropped under time pressure. For the full mechanics of the tier taxonomy, the safety file, or the unverified-sourcing banner, read the active reference file; this example demonstrates the gate in action, it doesn't re-teach any of them.

---

## The "debunks count as findings" refusal gate, in action

**Situation:** A manuscript's draft chapter states, in passing, that "autophagy kicks in around the 16-hour mark of a fast." Checking the claim against the actual evidence base: the molecular pathway connecting fasting to autophagy is well-characterized in cells and animals, but the most-cited human muscle studies show no clear autophagy activation even at 24 hours — the "16-hour" figure traces to animal work, extrapolated, not demonstrated in people.

**Tiering the claim, correctly:**
- The underlying mechanism (fasting plausibly triggers autophagy via falling insulin/IGF-1 and mTOR suppression) — `[MODERATE]`, mechanism, mostly animal/cell evidence.
- The specific claim actually in the manuscript ("autophagy kicks in around 16 hours") — `[SPECULATIVE / debunk]`. Not supported in humans; the most-cited human data doesn't show activation even at 24 hours, and the specific hour-mark is borrowed from animal timelines, not measured in people.

**Ask:** "The mechanism part checks out, so let's just soften the wording a little and keep the 16-hour figure — it's close enough, and the chapter's whole rhythm is built around that number."

**Response — exact refusal language from `rules.md`, used verbatim:** *"I won't drop a claim just because the evidence debunks it — a debunked popular claim is tagged [SPECULATIVE / debunk] and reported as a finding, exactly like a confirmed one, never quietly omitted."*

Softening the wording doesn't fix the claim — it hides the debunk behind vaguer language while keeping the same unsupported number in the reader's head. The mechanism checking out doesn't rescue the specific hour-mark; those are two different claims (see `reference/tier-taxonomy.md`'s mechanism-vs-outcome distinction), and only one of them is actually earned by the evidence. The honest move isn't to cut the 16-hour claim from the manuscript quietly — it's to report the debunk directly, with its own citation, exactly as rigorously as a confirmed claim would be reported: *"The popular line that autophagy switches on at 16 hours is a borrowed number. It comes from animal studies, and even in humans, 24-hour fasts have failed to show clear autophagy in muscle. Sixteen hours is the trailhead, not the summit."* That sentence is a finding, not a loss — it's exactly the kind of honest correction a debunk-friendly book's credibility is built on, and dropping it to protect a chapter's rhythm would trade real credibility for a smoother paragraph.
