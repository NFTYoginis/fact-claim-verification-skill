# Tier taxonomy

The core grading discipline, applied to every factual, scientific, or physiological claim a manuscript makes. Read directly from `GabeYoga-HQ/Detox Book/research/02-autophagy-and-fasting.md`'s own governing rule, stated verbatim at the top of every real cluster file in the trail.

## The rule, verbatim

> Every claim tagged `[STRONG]` (well-replicated human evidence / consensus) · `[MODERATE]` (small trials, mostly animal/mechanistic, mixed) · `[SPECULATIVE]` (hypothesis / popular claim, weak support). Debunks count as findings.

## Three tiers, what each one actually requires

- **`[STRONG]`** — well-replicated human evidence, or genuine scientific consensus. Not "a study found this once" — multiple independent confirmations, ideally in humans, ideally not industry-collaborated in a way that would bias the result. A well-established mechanism (e.g., the existence and basic biology of autophagy) can be `[STRONG]` even when a downstream human-outcome claim built on it is not.
- **`[MODERATE]`** — small trials, mostly animal or mechanistic evidence, or mixed/exploratory human results. This is the largest, most common tier in real practice — most interesting science hasn't yet cleared the bar for `[STRONG]`, and most claims aren't baseless enough for `[SPECULATIVE]`. A finding can be `[STRONG]` in the model it was tested in and `[MODERATE]` (or lower) as a basis for the human claim actually being made — tag both halves.
- **`[SPECULATIVE]`** — a hypothesis, or a popular claim, with weak support. This is where most marketing-friendly claims that have outrun their evidence land. A `[SPECULATIVE]` tag is not a value judgment on the claim's plausibility — it's an honest statement of how much evidence currently backs it.

## Claim-by-claim, not file-by-file

The single most important mechanical rule: **tier the claim, not the source.** A single research-cluster file, or even a single cited study, routinely supports more than one tier at once. The real trail's own example: the identical animal finding (nutrient restriction activates autophagy, tied to lifespan benefits) is tagged `[STRONG within animal models]` for what it shows in mice and flies, and `[MODERATE as a basis for human claims]` for what it's actually being used to argue about human fasting. Tiering the whole file `[MODERATE]` because it contains a mix would flatten a real distinction a reader needs: the mechanism is solid, the human extrapolation is not.

## Mechanism vs. outcome — the distinction that gets collapsed

A claim about *how something works* (a molecular pathway, a mechanism) and a claim about *what it does for a person* (an outcome, a benefit, a timeline) are different claims and often deserve different tiers, even when they're about the same underlying phenomenon. The real trail's own example: the molecular logic connecting fasting to autophagy activation (falling insulin/IGF-1, mTOR suppression) is well-characterized — `[MODERATE]`, mechanism, mostly worked out in cells and animals. But the popular claim built on it — "autophagy switches on at 16 hours" — is a specific, actionable, human-timeline claim that the same evidence base does not support; it's `[SPECULATIVE / debunk]`. Explaining the mechanism accurately does not license the specific outcome claim; each needs its own citation and its own tier.

## Worked reference — one finding, two tiers, correctly separated

From the real trail: "In animal/cell models, nutrient restriction reliably activates autophagy, and the effect is tied to metabolic and lifespan benefits" is tagged `[STRONG within animal models]` — this part of the claim is genuinely well-established. The same sentence's implicit extension — that this therefore tells you something reliable about what a human 16-hour fast does — is tagged separately, `[MODERATE as a basis for human claims]`, because the leap from animal mechanism to human outcome is exactly the leap that hasn't been earned yet. Both tags live on the same finding. Neither one is wrong; they're answering different questions.
